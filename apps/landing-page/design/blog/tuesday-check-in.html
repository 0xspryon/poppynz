The Tuesday check-in: what a good elderly visit looks like

Twenty minutes, a cup of tea and a short note home. A helper and a daughter describe the routine that works.

Robert is 81 and lives alone in Calgary. His daughter Danielle lives forty minutes away and works full time. Twice a week, on Tuesday and Friday mornings, a Poppynz helper named Jean lets herself in with the paper. We asked both of them what a visit actually looks like.

What a check-in is, and isn’t

An elderly check-in is a short, scheduled visit whose whole purpose is to confirm someone is all right and to leave them a little better than you found them. It’s company, a look around, and a note to the family. It isn’t personal care. Helpers on Poppynz don’t bathe, dress or lift, and they don’t give medication. If a family needs that, they need a PSW, and some of our helpers hold that certificate and list it on their profile.

A check-in includes

  • A hello at an agreed time, at the door or with a key
  • A look around: heating, food in the fridge, the phone working
  • Conversation, a hot drink, the paper, a short walk if wanted
  • A short written note to the family after each visit

A check-in doesn’t include

  • Personal care such as bathing or dressing
  • Giving or managing medication
  • Lifting, transfers or mobility support
  • Anything medical, ever

The routine that works

Jean’s visits run about twenty minutes, and they run the same way every time. “I put the kettle on before I say anything else,” she says. “While it boils I walk through the kitchen and the hall. Then we sit down and I lose the argument about the Flames.”

  1. Arrive at the same time. Robert knows it’s Jean at 9:30, not a stranger. Consistency is most of the comfort.
  2. Look before you talk. Is the house warm? Is there food that isn’t past its date? Any mail piling up, any new bruise, any smell of gas? Two minutes, quietly.
  3. Sit down. The visit is the conversation, not the checklist. Twenty minutes of real attention beats an hour of hovering.
  4. Leave a note. Before she goes, Jean writes three or four lines in the app for Danielle.

The note home

Danielle says the note is the whole reason she books the visits. “I don’t need a report. I need to know he ate, he was himself, and nothing looked off.” A good note is short, specific and boring: what he had for breakfast, what mood he was in, and anything that changed since last time. Jean’s note from a Tuesday in July read, in full: “Porridge, grumpy about the rain, new stack of unopened mail on the hall table, otherwise fine.”

I don’t need a report. I need to know he ate, he was himself, and nothing looked off. That’s all I wanted.

Danielle, Calgary

Setting it up on Poppynz

Check-ins are booked like any other service on Poppynz: the family and helper agree the details and put them into a written agreement before the first visit. For elder care, the agreement should be specific about a few things families often leave out.

  • The days and the time window, and who holds a key
  • What “fine” means for this person, and what to do if there’s no answer at the door
  • Who to call, in what order, if something is wrong
  • The rate per visit and whether a longer stay is billed by the hour

Helpers are matched by distance from the older person’s home, not the family’s, so a daughter in Calgary can book a helper for her father in Mississauga. Every helper has a Vulnerable Sector Check, which covers vulnerable adults as well as children, and families complete a short safety check too.

When a visit turns into a phone call

Once, Jean arrived to find Robert in his chair, confused and cold, with the furnace off. She didn’t write a note. She called Danielle, then stayed until she arrived. The agreement had the order of calls written down, so there was no deciding to do.

If a helper can’t get an answer at the door at the agreed time, the agreement should say what happens next: call the family first, then a neighbour, then emergency services. Write it down before it’s needed.
